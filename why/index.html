Why this catalog exists

Why end-of-life dates are worth tracking

A device that its manufacturer no longer updates is not a device that got old. It is a device whose known vulnerabilities are never closed, sitting where traffic enters a network — and the organizations that write national cybersecurity policy now treat that as the single most urgent hardware problem they have. This page states the case, names the priority set that case produces, and shows honestly how much of it this catalog covers today.

The evidence: national policy now treats unsupported edge devices as the priority

On 5 February 2026 CISA issued Binding Operational Directive 26-02, Mitigating Risk From End-of-Support Edge Devices, developed in coordination with OMB. Its Background says outright that unsupported devices "are often enabled by cyber campaigns that threaten both public and private sectors", that exploitation of them is "widespread", and that federal agencies "can remediate by following proven lifecycle management practices".

The directive is not advisory. It binds federal civilian agencies to decommission end-of-support edge devices on four deadlines — an inventory within three months, decommissioning within twelve, decommissioning everything within eighteen, and a standing discovery process within twenty-four — and it implements OMB Circular A-130, which requires that "unsupported information systems and system components are phased out as rapidly as possible". A federal agency that discovers an unsupported device has a dated obligation attached to it. That is the entire reason a date is worth publishing.

CISA, the FBI and the UK's National Cyber Security Centre say the same thing to everyone else in their joint fact sheet: nation-state actors "exploit end-of-support (EOS) edge devices—including, but not limited to, load balancers, firewalls, routers, and virtual private network (VPN) gateways—to gain network access, maintain presence, and compromise sensitive data", and they "urge all organizations" to act. CISA's Acting Director put it as a hygiene point rather than a security one: "Practicing good cyber hygiene starts with eliminating unsupported edge devices."

The commercial reading is the same argument. An unsupported device is one whose failures you can neither patch nor replace against a published schedule, and whose next failure arrives on an attacker's schedule instead of yours. A published end-of-life date converts that from a surprise into a line item.

What "end of support" means, in the words of the directive

CISA defines the condition without reference to a commercial milestone. Verbatim from the directive's Definitions section:

Hardware devices, firmware, and software versions that no longer receive timely, supported updates from the original equipment manufacturer, including patches for CVEs, security updates, software fixes (hotfixes), and defects.

Two consequences follow, and this catalog is built on both.

Unsupported is a status, not a published date. A device can be end of support while no vendor ever announced a day. The directive keys on update behaviour — "no longer receive timely, supported updates" — so a missing date does not mean a supported product. This is why every absent date on this site is published as unknown rather than quietly treated as "still supported", and why a row that leaves from a vendor's table is retained rather than deleted: a row disappearing is not an end of life, and neither is a row never appearing.

There is one condition here, not three. The directive uses "end of support" throughout; "end of life" and "end of sale" do not appear in it. It treats a product that is still for sale but no longer patched as in scope, and a product that cannot be bought but is still patched as out of scope. That is a defensible security boundary and a different one from this catalog's, which keeps end of sale, end of security support and end of life apart because vendors state them separately. Where a vendor states only one of them, this catalog publishes that one and leaves the others absent.

The shape of that table is the finding. The vendors carrying it, in order, are Juniper Networks (3499), Cisco (772), WatchGuard (170), and the classes with no records at all are the next coverage work — not because their dates are unknown, but because those vendors publish them behind a login, a web-application firewall, or a page that renders client-side. Coverage here follows whichever vendor published a page a collector can read, not which device is most exposed.

What CISA has committed to, and why it is not a source here yet

The same directive promises a dataset this catalog could use directly. CISA Action 1, verbatim: "CISA will provide an initial list of EOS and soon-to-be EOS edge devices for Required Actions #2 and #3. This list will include the IT product name, version number, and end of support date."

Product name, version number and an end-of-support date is exactly a release row. The list is not published. It is named in the directive as plain text with no link, no attachment and no path; it is absent from both of CISA's sitemaps, from CISA's own site search and from CISA's GitHub organisation; the directive requires no publication by anyone, since every data obligation in it runs from agencies to CISA; and CISA itself calls the list "a preliminary repository" that agencies should stop relying on after three months, because after that they are expected to find their own end-of-support devices.

So nothing is ingested from it, and nothing is written to look as though it were. Two other CISA artifacts were checked and rejected for this purpose: the Known Exploited Vulnerabilities feed is the only machine-readable feed CISA publishes and carries no version and no lifecycle field at all, and its advisory text occasionally contains the hedged phrase "could be end-of-life" about Linux kernel branches, which is not a lifecycle claim. The CISA advisory feeds can express end-of-life in their schema but do not populate it.

If CISA publishes that list with a citation to each vendor notice behind a date, it becomes a legitimate registered source — and the fifteen vendors with no records here would become the natural first use of it, because for those it would be the only source available. Until then, the vendors' own pages are the way in.

What this catalog will not publish

The value of a date is entirely in whether it is real, so the rules that keep these dates real are worth stating in public. Every milestone on this site is one of four things, and nothing else is published as a date:

  • A date the vendor stated, at the precision the vendor stated it. A month stays a month; it is never padded into a day.
  • A date CISA stated, attributed to CISA rather than to the vendor, where a government source is the one that published it.
  • A date a vendor's own explicit rule derives — a stated support duration or a named release trigger — labelled derived everywhere it appears, recomputed on every build, and excluded from the exact-day feeds and the OpenEoX export.
  • Nothing. Where a vendor has not published a date, this catalog publishes none, and says so.

Specifically, this catalog never publishes a date inferred from a release cadence, from a version number, from the age of a product, from the fact that a newer release exists, or from a product's absence from a table. A generic support window never becomes an end-of-security-support date. When two sources disagree about one date, that is published as a review to be resolved, not merged into a value both sources would have to be wrong about.
